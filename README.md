**Use this when** a specific person has accepted and you have not yet decided
what they will own alone by day 90.

## The rule

> A plan with no failure signal is a reading list.
> Every mark says what good looks like and what not good looks like.

Most onboarding plans are a meeting schedule and a document list. They produce
a day 91 where nobody can say whether it worked, so the answer defaults to
"seems fine" until month six makes it obvious.

## How it works

**1. I name the one thing they own alone at day 90**, then build the weeks
backward from it. One thing. If you name three, I tell you which one survives
and why the other two are month six.

**2. I put the fixed calendar in first.** Legal and payroll dates are not
negotiable and they eat week one. Form I-9 Section 1 is due on or before the
first day of work for pay, and the employer side within three business days of
the start date. Verify current rules against the USCIS Handbook for Employers
at https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/handbook-for-employers-m-274. Then your
payroll cutoff, your benefits enrollment window from the plan document, and
the lead time on equipment, keys, and account access.

**3. Three phases, not thirty tasks.** Weeks 1 to 2 watch. Weeks 3 to 6 do it
with review. Weeks 7 to 12 own it, with you checking output instead of steps.

**4. Every mark gets a not-good line.** Written now, while you are calm, so at
week six you are comparing against a standard instead of against your mood.

**5. I budget your hours, out loud.** Week one is expensive. If you cannot name
the hours, the plan is fiction and I say so rather than scheduling around you.

**6. I label what I know about this person.** Resume and interview notes are
`FROM YOU` with a quote. Everything else is `GUESSED`. I will not invent their
background, their motivation, or what they struggled with at the last job.

## The grid

| phase | they are doing | you are checking | failure signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| wk 1 to 2 | watching, writing down what breaks | that they ask questions | silent and agreeable |
| wk 3 to 6 | doing it, you review after | the review volume dropping | same correction twice |
| wk 7 to 12 | owning it, you see output | the output, on a cadence | you still find out first |

```
   CHECKED the I-9 timing against USCIS guidance, Section 1 on or
   before day one and the employer side within three business days.
   GUESSED the access lead time. Ask your billing tool admin today,

Q  How many hours do you have in week one?

   GUESSED nothing. Load bearing. At under six hours this becomes a

Q  What does not-good look like at week six?
A  You are correcting the same invoice error a second time, or they

   CHECKED against the grid, both are observable without a meeting.

6 answers. 3 guessed, 2 lookups handed back.
Give me their name and your week one hours, or say go.
```

Then the week by week. One page.

## Limits

The trap: a plan written for the role instead of the person. The part they
already did for four years gets three weeks of shadowing, and the part they
have never done gets one afternoon, because the plan never asked what they
actually came in knowing.

Second trap: tasks with no owner on your side. "Gets access to the billing
tool" sits in week one of every plan and is the single most common reason week
two is wasted.

This cannot know your provisioning bottlenecks, your plan documents, or your
actual calendar, so those stay labeled until you fill them.

It is not legal or immigration advice. Hiring paperwork rules change and vary,
so the dates above are a prompt to verify, not a compliance check.

It also will not tell you whether to keep someone at day 90. It gives you the
evidence to decide that yourself, which is the part a plan can honestly do.

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
